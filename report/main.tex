In Bayesian inference and model comparison, a central computational task is to characterize a posterior distribution over a parameter vector $\mathbf{x} \in E \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ while simultaneously evaluating its normalizing constant (or marginal likelihood). Specifically, given a prior density $\eta$ and a non-negative likelihood function $\mathcal{L}: E \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$, we consider the posterior density
\begin{equation}\label{eq:post_full}
    \pi(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\gamma(\mathbf{x})}{Z} = \frac{\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x})\eta(\mathbf{x})}{Z},
\end{equation}
where $\gamma(\mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x})\eta(\mathbf{x})$ is the unnormalized target density and
\begin{equation}
    Z = \int_E \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x})\eta(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
\end{equation}
is the normalizing constant. To estimate these two quantities, Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) is commonly used. 
In particular, one simulates an ergodic Markov chain with invariant density $\pi$, yielding approximate draws from $\pi$ after a sufficiently long burn-in phase.
Then $\pi(\mathbf{x})$ can be estimated by the empirical distribution of these samples. 
Despite its widespread success, MCMC can struggle to explore complex or multimodal posterior distributions effectively.
This motivates the development of Nested Sampling (NS) and Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC). 

NS is proposed by \cite{10.1214/06-BA127} to estimate the normalizing constant $Z$  by maintaining an ensemble of sample points, and generating new points from progressively constrained or nested versions of prior. 
Specifically, 
However, one of the significant drawback of NS is that it requires of generating perfect and independent samples from the constrained distributions at each iteration. 
% Problem with NS -> Solution

SMC samplers, introduced by \cite{10.1111/j.1467-9868.2006.00553.x}, are a general methodology that traverses a population of particles through a sequence of distributions using reweighting, resampling, and mutation steps. 
We focus on the case where all sampling distributions live on the same space $E$. 
One can sample from the density $\pi$ by introducing a sequence of intermediate targets that smoothly bridge the prior and posterior densities via an inverse temperature schedule $0 = \delta_0 < \dots < \delta_T = 1$, given by $\pi_t(\mathbf{x}) \propto \eta(\mathbf{x})\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x})^{\delta_t}$.

\cite{10.1093/jrsssb/qkaf015} proposed two NS-based SMC algorithms, NS-SMC and adaptive NS-SMC. 
These two algorithms are derived differently from the original NS and do not assume independent samples are produced at each iteration as in the origin NS algorithm.

\subsection{Critical Analysis and Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sensitivity to the adaptation parameter $\alpha$: While the paper suggests several candidate values for $\alpha$, it remains unclear how different choices affect the resulting threshold schedule and the variance or accuracy of the log-evidence estimator.
    \item Choice and coverage of the prior $\eta(\mathbf{x})$: Selecting an initial distribution $\eta(\mathbf{x})$ that adequately covers the parameter space---particularly high-likelihood regions---is crucial, as poor coverage can degrade sampling efficiency.
    \item Sensitivity to proposal step size: In the spike-and-slab experiment, the choice of step size in the proposal distribution has a significant impact on the empirical performance of ATA-SMC.
    \item Likelihood plateaus: Similar to standard nested sampling, ANS-SMC and NS-SMC may encounter difficulties when the likelihood function exhibits flat regions or plateaus \citep{10.1214/23-SS144}.
\end{itemize}
